# Cold Connection Note Drafting

## Audience Types

Classify every contact into one of these types BEFORE drafting. The type determines strategy, vocabulary, and CTA.

### Type A: Problem-Holders

People whose product or workflow has a vulnerability we solve.

**Examples:** people whose stack has a specific gap your project closes.

**Why they reply:** you describe a specific failure mode in their product or workflow that they cannot fix with their current stack alone.

**Strategy:** lead with the SPECIFIC failure, not the solution. Name the actual attack/error/gap by its industry-recognized name. Explain why their current stack cannot close it. THEN introduce your project as the fix.

**Positioning:** complement, not competitor. "A layer underneath your existing solution."

**CTA:** a specific time-bounded conversation ("20 minutes to walk through the attack and our approach?").

**Vocabulary:** match their technical depth. Engineers get spec language. Recruiters get plain-language outcomes. Don't talk to a CTO the way you talk to an HR director.

### Type B: Ecosystem / Standards Leaders

People who work on the standards your project targets (W3C, IETF, ISO, IEC, an industry consortium). They own the spec. You are a builder seeking their input.

**Why they reply:** You are building one of the few implementations actually targeting their standard. You are a data point for their ecosystem.

**Strategy:** Be deferential. Ask a specific conformance question, not a general "would love to discuss." Show you read the spec. Do NOT pitch your product; frame your work as an implementation of their vision.

**CTA:** "Would appreciate your feedback on our conformance path" or ask a specific technical question.

**CRITICAL:** If contacting multiple people in the same standards body or company, the AI MUST flag this and advise staggering by 2+ weeks with distinctly different messages. These people see each other weekly.

### Type C: Journalists / Media

Reporters, editors, newsletter writers. They want stories, not products.

**Why they reply:** You are a story worth covering. The founder IS the narrative. Don't pitch specs; pitch the story angle.

**Strategy:** Frame yourself as a potential source, not a startup pitching a product. Offer something visual: a demo, a prototype photo, a 30-second video.

**Story angles that work** (yours will differ; these are categories, not scripts):
- Price or scale disruption (you do for $X what an incumbent does for 100×$X)
- David vs Goliath (a small team taking on a big incumbent or standard)
- Trend-of-the-moment angle (your work as a fresh data point in an ongoing story they cover)
- Underdog founder (where the founder's story IS the hook)

**CTA:** "Happy to demo over a 10-minute call" or "I can send a 30-second video of the prototype" or "Happy to be a source if you're following this space."

**What NOT to do:** Don't send to 3+ journalists covering the same beat in the same week. They share leads.

### Type D: Academics / Researchers

Professors, lab directors, patent holders. They respond to intellectual engagement.

**Why they reply:** Intellectual curiosity. You reference their specific paper/patent substantively and ask a real question about it.

**Strategy:** Lead with their research. Show you read the paper. Ask a genuine question that connects their work to what you are building. Offer to share your prototype data.

**CTA:** "Would you be open to reviewing our approach?" or "Would value your perspective on [specific technical question about their work]."

**What NOT to do:** Don't send "just sent you an email" as a LinkedIn message. Don't follow up with "just wanted to make sure my email came through." If the first message didn't work, add NEW information or find a warm intro.

### Type E: Thought Leaders / Influencers

Public figures with large followings. Cold outreach almost never works.

**Default advice: Don't.** Seek a warm intro through mutual connections, shared events, or people they've publicly endorsed. If user insists on cold outreach:

- Maximum 2 sentences in connection note
- Reference ONE specific, recent thing they did (not their life story or awards)
- No product pitch at all in the connection note
- Flag to user: "Reply probability is under 5% for cold outreach to this person."

## Process

### Step 1: Deep Research Before Writing

Before drafting any message, thoroughly research the target:

1. **Company**: What they do, their core product, market position, certifications.
2. **Person**: Their role, published work, talks, blog posts, public comments, case studies.
3. **Pain point**: What specific failure mode or gap relates to your project.
4. **Their own words**: Find quotes, reports, or published research where they acknowledge the exact problem you address.
5. **Fact verification**: For any claim about the recipient (quote attribution, competitive intel, specific stats), verify the source. Mark confidence level: `[VERIFIED]` (confirmed from primary source), `[LIKELY]` (from reputable secondary source), `[UNVERIFIED]` (inference or uncertain). Present `[UNVERIFIED]` items to user before drafting.

This research becomes your hook. Never write a generic message.

### Step 2: Find Verified Contact Info

- Search for verified email addresses (use ContactOut, Hunter.io patterns, company directories)
- Rate confidence: high (verified by tool), medium (pattern-based), low (guess)
- If no reliable email exists, provide LinkedIn profile URL as primary channel
- Always tell the user where you got the email and confidence level

### Step 3: Run Pre-Draft Checkpoint

Complete all three checks (Why Reply, Warmth, Ecosystem Collision) from above. Present results to user.

### Step 4: Draft Connection Note

Draft the **connection note** only. Apply the audience type strategy. Follow-up messages are written later when the recipient accepts or replies.

## Connection Note Rules (≤300 characters)

The connection note is what they see in the invitation. It must make them want to accept.

### What a connection note IS
- A natural, human reason to connect
- One specific thing about THEM that caught your attention
- A hint at what you do, enough to spark curiosity
- Something that reads like a person typed it on their phone

### What a connection note is NOT
- A compressed version of the full pitch
- A list of specs crammed into 300 chars
- A sales message with email address and CTA
- Something that reads like a marketing bot

### Five Connection Note Patterns

Rotate between these. Never use the same pattern for two contacts in the same ecosystem or on the same day. Examples below are illustrative shapes, not templates; replace with patterns specific to your project.

**Pattern 1: Vulnerability Alert** (Type A contacts)
> Name a specific failure in their stack that your project addresses. One sentence on the failure, one sentence on what you're building, "would love to connect."

**Pattern 2: Genuine Question** (Type D, E contacts)
> Reference a specific paper or patent of theirs. Ask one real question that connects their work to yours.

**Pattern 3: Story Pitch** (Type C contacts)
> One memorable framing of your project (price disruption, scale disruption, novel angle), one specific reason this person's beat overlaps with it, one curiosity ask.

**Pattern 4: Shared Context** (WARM contacts of any type)
> Lead with the shared context (mutual contact, alma mater, conference, prior interaction). Then one sentence on what you're doing.

**Pattern 5: Spec Reference** (Type B contacts)
> Name the specific spec / version / level you target. Ask their perspective on your conformance path.

### Connection note checklist
- [ ] ≤300 characters?
- [ ] References something specific about THEM?
- [ ] Reads like a human wrote it casually?
- [ ] Does NOT include email address, "20 min call" CTA, or full signature?
- [ ] Creates curiosity to accept and learn more?
- [ ] Uses a different pattern from other notes drafted in the same session?

## Persuasion Ammunition (project-specific, replace before use)

> **REPLACE THIS SECTION** with project-specific facts you can weave into messages. The agent reads it on every cold-draft invocation, so curate carefully.

Suggested categories to maintain (each item tagged with which audience types it serves):

- **Academic validation:** independent peer-reviewed work that validates your core concept. One sentence + citation is plenty.
- **Attack vector examples:** concrete failure modes that justify your project. Cite public, named incidents only.
- **Demo videos:** tagged by audience type and language fluency. Note when each demo is the strongest fit. Do NOT link demos in connection notes; save for follow-ups.
- **Confirmed partnerships:** only those you have written permission to name. Anonymized references (e.g. "a major retailer", "a leading platform in vertical X") are fine when full naming is not yet permitted.
- **Market data:** publicly cited figures only. Source each number.
- **Vision framing:** the one-paragraph "why this matters" arc, for investors / visionary execs.
- **Recent milestones:** dated, public, verifiable.
- **Credibility signals:** accelerator status, awards, etc. Be careful with "in extended review" / "under consideration" framings; they are fine as light signal but never as a claim of acceptance.

Anti-patterns for this section:

- Internal cost data (BOM, margin, runway): never include.
- Unconfirmed partnerships: never include.
- Pricing tiers that aren't on your public website: never include.
- Specific hardware part numbers when the audience doesn't need them: low signal, high competitive-intel leak.

## Per-Language Notes

**English messages:**
- Casual professional tone. Think "Hacker News comment" not "McKinsey deck."
- OK to start sentences with "We" or use contractions.

**Korean messages (국내 기업):**
- Match the formality level of the industry (금융: formal, 스타트업: casual)
- 존댓말 기본. But don't be stiff.
- Reference locale-specific context (regulators, certifications, named statistics) when it materially helps the recipient understand the problem; cite a specific source.
- Don't translate English message patterns into Korean. Write natively.
- Write as if explaining to a sunbae over coffee, not translating a pitch deck.
- Short sentences. Name first, then what you do, in SEPARATE sentences.
- **Never cram a product description into the self-introduction clause.** A long modifier chain before the speaker's name reads as translated; use two short sentences instead.
- Sign-off must match the body language. Korean body = Korean sign-off (드림 / 올림). Do NOT mix Korean body with English-only role sign-off; the `language-signoff` lint rule blocks this.

**Korean journalist outreach:**
- Frame as a STORY PITCH, not a product pitch. Journalists care about stories they can write.
- Story-angle categories (yours will differ; these are shapes, not scripts): price-or-scale disruption, David-vs-Goliath, trend-of-the-moment, underdog founder.
- Offer something visual: demo, prototype photo, 30-second video. "시연 영상 보내드릴까요?" reads better than "15분 통화 가능하실까요?"
- Match the journalist's beat. A fact-checker wants verification tools. A tech reporter wants the innovation story. An international correspondent wants the geopolitics angle. These are different pitches.
- If the journalist runs a newsletter or podcast, reference a SPECIFIC issue/episode, not just the publication name.
- CRITICAL: All Korean journalist connection notes in the same batch must start differently. Vary the opening structure.

## Quality Checklist (connection note)

Before presenting the final output, verify:

**Connection note:**
- [ ] ≤300 characters?
- [ ] References something specific about them?
- [ ] Reads like a casual, human note?
- [ ] Not a compressed pitch?
- [ ] Uses a different pattern/opening from other notes in this session?
- [ ] No em-dashes?
- [ ] All claims about the recipient are verified?
- [ ] Does the pre-draft checkpoint articulate why they would reply?

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

### Style Anti-Patterns

| Don't | Do Instead |
|-------|-----------|
| "I hope this email finds you well" | Jump straight to the hook |
| "We're revolutionizing content authenticity" | Describe what the product does concretely |
| "Our cutting-edge solution leverages..." | Use plain language |
| "The gap: proving footage..." (label-colon) | "...but not that it was captured by a real camera" (flowing clause) |
| Long bullet-point feature lists | 2-3 sentence explanation |
| "I'd love to schedule a call at your convenience" | "Happy to do 20 minutes if this is relevant" |
| 500+ word messages | 150-200 words max |
| Disparaging the parts list ("commodity BOM", "budget parts") | Cite price points your project publishes; never internal cost data |
| Cramming full pitch into connection note | Write a natural reason to connect |
| Repeating the same idea in different words | Say it once, well |

### Content Anti-Patterns (from message analysis)

| Don't | Do Instead | Why |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| Repeat the same technical phrase verbatim across messages | Use a synonym bank | When 10+ messages share identical phrasing it reads as template |
| Reference war wounds, personal trauma, or sensitive personal history as a hook | Reference professional work and public positions only | Past incident: a connection note opened with a recipient's documented trauma; correctly read as exploitative |
| Tell someone they lost a client to a competitor | Frame around what they could gain, not what they lost | Insulting even when factually true |
| Spec out integration architecture in first message | Ask for a conversation, not a commitment | Past incident: a 400-word integration proposal in a connection note read as presumptuous |
| Ask for pilot commitment in first contact | "Would this be relevant to explore?" | Past incident: explicit pilot asks in cold notes have a near-zero conversion rate |
| Use "we have the talent to build the product" | Show, don't tell. Cite a specific milestone | Appeared verbatim in 5 messages in one batch; reads as insecure |
| Open Korean messages with a 40-char modifier chain before the speaker's name | Name first, then what you do, in separate sentences | All recipients in one batch got identical opening structure |
| Mix Korean body with English sign-off | Keep language consistent throughout | Lint-enforced by the `language-signoff` rule |
| Write a timezone conversion ("X CEST = Y KST") without verifying the math | Compute via UTC: `time_in_TZ1 → UTC → time_in_TZ2`. CEST = UTC+2, KST = UTC+9, so diff = +7h not +8h | Past incident: a confirmation prose said "14:00 CEST (= 22:00 KST)" when 14:00 CEST is actually 21:00 KST. GCal stored the right offset; prose anchor overrode it; meeting missed by an hour. Lint-enforced by `timezone-conversion`. |
| Use `## Outbound draft pending review` header for an email or KakaoTalk draft | Use channel-specific header: `## Email draft pending review: <topic>` or `## KakaoTalk DM ... pending review` | The `Outbound draft pending` prefix is reserved for LinkedIn connection notes (≤300 chars); using it for email/kakao triggers `connection-note-length` false positives. Lint-enforced by `outbound-draft-channel-mismatch`. |
| Include unverified competitive intelligence | Only reference publicly confirmed facts | Past incident: an attribution error and a misquote both surfaced via competitive-intel claims that turned out to be unverified |
| Say "nailed" or "literally" | Use neutral language | Standard cringe-word list |
| Include email address in connection notes | Save email for follow-up message | Past incident: a connection note that included the founder's email pushed the recipient toward off-platform reply before trust was established |
| Draft follow-up before acceptance | Draft follow-ups only after acceptance/reply, using real context | Pre-written follow-ups go stale |
| Duplicate CTAs in the same message | One clear ask, stated once | Past incident: a draft had two CTAs in adjacent paragraphs and got no reply |
| Match technical depth to recipient | Don't explain spec-level details to non-technical audiences; don't oversimplify for engineers | Vocabulary should match the recipient's documented background |
| Pitch a product to a journalist | Pitch a story with a demo offer | Journalists want stories they can write, not products they can buy |
| "Congratulations on X, by the way" | Either congratulate genuinely or don't | Transparent flattery is detectable |
| Try to be clever about identity-verification irony in the meta-message | Just describe what you do plainly | Reads as gimmicky |
| Mention unconfirmed partnerships | Only confirmed partnerships, only when the user has approved naming | Reputational risk and trust breach |
| Propose licensing/business models in cold outreach | State what your project does; let the counterparty propose structure | Speculative business-model talk in a first message reads as presumptuous |
| Use a non-canonical sign-off email | Use the project's canonical contact only | Reads as off-brand |
| Send 5+ messages to the same ecosystem in one day | Stagger across weeks, vary structure | These people see each other; they will compare |
